# Topic: AI Extends Human Cognition
# Phase: 3
# Learning Goal
 How does repeated AI use reshape human cognitive skills and long-term cognitive development?

# Reading Material
Throughout history, technologies have changed which cognitive skills human practice. Writing reduced the need to preserve information entirely through memory; calculators reduced routine arithmetic; search engines changed how people retrieve facts. AI may represent another redistribution of cognitive labor rather than a general deterioration of human ability.
A crucial distinction is between learned skills and basic cognitive abilities. Learned skills depend heavily on repeated practice and domain-specific knowledge, whereas abilities such as working memory and selective attention support performance across many tasks. Cash et al. (2026) argue that skills repeatedly delegated to AI may change or weaken, but current evidence does not justify the broader claim that AI use necessarily reduces basic cognitive capacity.
At the same time, AI creates new demands. When a system can generate information quickly, human expertise increasingly involves deciding what to ask, evaluating whether an answer is reliable, detecting errors, comparing alternatives, integrating information, and deciding when AI should be trusted. Cognitive effort therefore does not simply disappear; its target changes.
Evidence that AI-assisted practice can improve later unaided performance also challenges the idea that reliance on AI must undermine skill development (Lira et al., 2026). When people remain actively involved in evaluating and using AI outputs, AI can support rather than eliminate learning.
Long-term AI use may therefore produce cognitive adaptation: some routine operations become externalized while humans develop complementary skills for monitoring, evaluation, synthesis, and decision making. The future of cognition may be defined less by what humans can do alone than by how effectively they think with intelligent tools.
